\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The construction is best read as a structural complement to the existing alignment-research stack, not as a replacement for it. We sketch the points of contact.

\paragraph{Constitutional AI and constitutional admission.}
Constitutional AI~\cite{baiConstitutionalAI2022} encodes a constitution into the model's training signal: a written list of principles is used to score candidate outputs, and the policy is shaped to prefer outputs the constitution endorses. The constitution lives inside the model, as preferences. An admission-layer construction encodes a constitution into the substrate's typed admission predicate: a list of structural commitments is enforced as preconditions for the substrate to execute a tool call. The constitution lives outside the model, as type checks. The two constitutions are not the same object and do not interfere; they discipline different points in the action pipeline. The first shapes what the model wants; the second shapes what the substrate is willing to execute.

The constructions compose without contradiction. Constitutional AI cannot guarantee that a model never produces a destructive tool call; it shapes the prior. An admission-layer construction can guarantee that a destructive tool call is not executable without the typed witness. Together, the model is less likely to produce the inadmissible call and the substrate is unwilling to execute it.

\paragraph{Debate, scalable oversight, and the verdict-versus-admission distinction.}
Debate~\cite{christianoDebate2018} and scalable oversight~\cite{leikeScalableOversight2018,bowmanScalableOversight2022} produce verdicts on candidate outputs that exceed the operator's direct capacity to judge. A verdict is a yes-or-no judgement about whether a given output is safe to act on. Admission is a different operation: it accepts or rejects an action envelope against a typed predicate. A verdict says ``the model's output is acceptable'' or ``the model's output is unsafe''. Admission says ``the substrate will execute this typed call'' or ``the substrate will not execute this typed call, because its envelope does not satisfy the structural predicate''. The two operations can be stacked: a verdict-based oversight system can serve as a precondition on admission, and an admission predicate can refuse calls that no verdict permits. The substrate does not require the verdict to exist for admission to be sound; admission is a structural check whose correctness does not depend on the oversight loop.

\paragraph{Red-teaming and the substrate-layer dual.}
Red-teaming~\cite{perezRedTeaming2022} attempts to elicit failure modes from the deployed model before release. A substrate that admits calls on structural grounds is the dual: where red-teaming asks ``what failure can we elicit from the model'', the substrate asks ``what failure does the typed envelope refuse to admit regardless of what the model produces''. The two questions are complementary. Red-teaming exposes the model's failure surface; the substrate-layer construction reduces the failure surface by refusing classes of failure at admission. A red-team that elicits a destructive irreversible tool call from a misaligned model finds a model-side failure; the substrate's admission of that call would require a typed rollback witness or a bilateral cosignature the model cannot supply, so the operational consequence of the model-side failure is bounded by the structural refusal.

\paragraph{Responsible Scaling Policies and the runtime artifact.}
Frontier-safety frameworks such as Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy~\cite{anthropicRSP2023} commit to capability-gated deployment thresholds. These commitments are pre-deployment instruments: they specify what evaluations a model must pass before it is allowed to be deployed in a given mode. They do not produce a runtime artifact that an external party can replay to check what a deployed model actually did. A substrate that produces canonically-signed admission receipts gives the RSP commitment a post-deployment counterpart: the deployer can show that every destructive call admitted by the substrate carried the structural witnesses the RSP threshold required, and the operator (or a third-party auditor) can replay the receipt corpus to check the claim. The post-deployment runtime artifact is the missing complement to the pre-deployment policy commitment.

\paragraph{Composition is the headline.}
The discussion converges on one structural point. Model-side safety and substrate-side safety are not competing programmes. They are orthogonal layers in a single safety construction, and the literature would benefit from naming the second layer explicitly. Naming it produces a clearer specification of what each layer is responsible for: training shapes the model's preferences; admission refuses inadmissible envelopes; oversight produces verdicts on candidate outputs; the substrate produces receipts on executed actions. Each layer has a precise scope; the safety property of the system is the composition.
