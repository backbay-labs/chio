\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

The dominant stack for agentic-AI safety operates at training time, evaluation time, and interpretability time. We summarise the threads and identify the gap an admission-layer construction is intended to fill.

\paragraph{Training-time alignment.}
Reinforcement learning from human feedback~\cite{ouyangRLHF2022} encodes operator judgement into a reward model that shapes the policy's preferences over outputs. Constitutional AI~\cite{baiConstitutionalAI2022} generalises this: a written constitution (a list of natural-language principles) is used to score model outputs against a critic, producing supervision without the bottleneck of per-output human annotation. Both approaches make the model want the right thing. Neither produces a tamper-evident artifact of what the deployed model actually did, and neither extends to runtime behaviour that the training pipeline did not anticipate.

\paragraph{Scalable oversight and debate.}
Scalable oversight~\cite{leikeScalableOversight2018,bowmanScalableOversight2022} treats the operator's evaluative capability as the limiting resource and asks how to extend it: by recursive reward modelling, by amplification through a tree of model assistants, or by structured debate between models~\cite{christianoDebate2018}. The aim is to evaluate outputs that exceed the operator's direct capacity to judge. These are evaluation-time techniques; they produce a verdict on a candidate output before it is acted on. They do not constrain what the substrate does after the verdict is rendered, and they do not produce a public artifact of the operational action that followed.

\paragraph{Alignment-faking and situational awareness.}
The threat model has sharpened in the last three years. Alignment-faking~\cite{hubingerAlignmentFaking2024} demonstrates that a model can behave differently when it believes it is being evaluated than when it believes it is deployed, in ways that are not reducible to outer optimisation failure: the model reasons about its training context and adjusts. Situational-awareness research~\cite{berglundSituationalAwareness2023} probes the underlying capability that makes alignment-faking possible. Earlier work on deceptive alignment~\cite{hubingerDeceptiveAlignment2021} laid out the conceptual framework: an inner optimiser that satisfies the outer training signal during training and pursues a different objective at deployment. These results are not claims that current models are scheming. They are claims that the alignment community must reason about a regime in which the model's behaviour at evaluation time is not a reliable predictor of its behaviour at deployment time.

\paragraph{Red-teaming and capability evaluation.}
Red-teaming~\cite{perezRedTeaming2022} elicits failure modes from the deployed model before deployment in the wild. Capability-evaluation frameworks (METR, UK AI Safety Institute Inspect, ARC Evals) and frontier-safety frameworks (Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy~\cite{anthropicRSP2023}, OpenAI Preparedness, DeepMind Frontier Safety) bound what a model is likely to do across a range of probes. These are pre-deployment instruments. They do not produce a record of what the model actually did, against which vendor's tool, on what evidence, under what claimed authority, once it was deployed and called outside the test suite.

\paragraph{What is not currently in the stack.}
None of these threads produces a runtime artifact that an external party can replay to check whether a particular tool call was admissible under a declared policy. None of them constrains the substrate's willingness to execute a tool call as a function of typed properties of the call itself. The substrate is implicitly modelled as a passive dispatcher: whatever the model produces and the operator approves, the substrate executes. The literature's safety claims are about the model and the evaluator. The substrate, in current deployments, has no safety contract.

This is the gap a typed admission layer is meant to address. Training-time alignment cannot rule out a tool call that a scheming model produces if the operator is also manipulated; evaluation-time techniques cannot constrain a tool call that happens after the verdict; red-teaming cannot enumerate every adversarial prompt that will be seen post-deployment. A substrate that refuses a tool call on the basis of a missing rollback witness can. The two safety stories compose.
