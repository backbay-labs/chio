\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

The dominant stack for agentic-AI safety operates at training time, evaluation time, and interpretability time. We summarise the threads and identify the gap an admission-layer construction fills.

\paragraph{Training-time alignment.}
RLHF~\cite{ouyangRLHF2022} encodes operator judgement into a reward model that shapes the policy's preferences over outputs. Constitutional AI~\cite{baiConstitutionalAI2022} generalises this: a written constitution scores outputs through a critic, producing supervision without per-output annotation. Both approaches make the model want the right thing. Neither produces a tamper-evident artifact of what the deployed model actually did, and neither extends to runtime behaviour the training pipeline did not anticipate.

\paragraph{Scalable oversight and debate.}
Scalable oversight~\cite{leikeScalableOversight2018,bowmanScalableOversight2022} treats the operator's evaluative capability as the limiting resource and extends it through recursive reward modelling, amplification, or debate between models~\cite{christianoDebate2018}. These are evaluation-time techniques; they produce a verdict on a candidate output before it is acted on. They do not constrain what the substrate does after the verdict.

\paragraph{Alignment-faking and situational awareness.}
The threat model has sharpened. Alignment-faking~\cite{hubingerAlignmentFaking2024} demonstrates that a model can behave differently when it believes it is being evaluated than when it believes it is deployed, in ways not reducible to outer optimisation failure. Situational-awareness research~\cite{berglundSituationalAwareness2023} probes the underlying capability. Earlier work on deceptive alignment~\cite{hubingerDeceptiveAlignment2021} laid out the conceptual frame. These results are not claims that current models scheme. They are claims that the community must reason about a regime in which evaluation-time behaviour is not a reliable predictor of deployment-time behaviour.

\paragraph{Red-teaming and capability evaluation.}
Red-teaming~\cite{perezRedTeaming2022} elicits failure modes before deployment. Frontier-safety frameworks such as the Responsible Scaling Policy~\cite{anthropicRSP2023} bound deployed capability with capability-gated thresholds. These are pre-deployment instruments. They do not produce a record of what the model actually did, against which tool, under what authority, once deployed.

\paragraph{The gap.}
None of these threads produces a runtime artifact an external party can replay to check whether a particular tool call was admissible under a declared policy. None constrains the substrate's willingness to execute a tool call as a function of typed properties of the call itself. The substrate is implicitly modelled as a passive dispatcher: whatever the model produces and the operator approves, the substrate executes. The safety claims in the literature are about the model and the evaluator; the substrate has no safety contract. A typed admission layer is intended to close this gap. The two safety stories compose: training-time alignment shapes what the model wants, and admission-time discipline shapes what the substrate is willing to execute.
