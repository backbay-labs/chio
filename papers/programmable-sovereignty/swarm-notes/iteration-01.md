## Persona 1: Hostile NDSS reviewer

The single most damaging new observation is that the paper now stakes its strongest defensible claim on a Hartian "constructive instance of the rule of recognition" (\S7 line 10, conclusion line 3) while the formal artifact it cites for that claim has been openly demoted in \S1 to "two definitional refinement bridges" (\S1 bullet 2) whose work is "to anchor the type-level invariant" (\S4 line 71): the rule of recognition, in other words, is recognized in this paper by `rfl`.

That tension survives five passes and is now load-bearing in three places at once. (1) Abstract: "amendment enactment is conditioned at the type level on a refinement witness and is unconstructable in the well-typed runtime path without one" reads as a theorem; \S4 line 71 concedes it is a typing-discipline observation discharged definitionally. A PC member skimming the abstract is being sold a result the body retracts. (2) \S7 line 10: the Hart framing leans on the claim that any party can "replay" the predicate "over the same canonical bytes." But \S9 bullet 3 (portability) admits Chio receipt schemas are Chio-specific with no published interop profile, and the polish-diff's "findings recorded but NOT applied" section #5 records that `polityAdmits` decidability is a category error (Lean treats `ReceiptId -> Bool` as opaque). The Hartian replay test the paper sells is, by the paper's own admission elsewhere, not yet portable and not yet decidable in the model. (3) Conclusion line 3: "the next concrete result the substrate enables is bilateral admission against an external regulator's co-signing key" -- yet \S9 bullet "Regulator accreditation gap" admits no recognized framework cites Lean-attested receipts. The next-step claim and the limitation bullet contradict one another in fewer than two pages.

The AI safety paragraph (\S7 line 22, added pass 5) introduces a fresh overclaim: "a scheming agent cannot retroactively deny a cross-vendor call's predicate set." This conflates *binding* (receipts canonically pin predicate identity, true) with *non-repudiation under adversarial key custody* (not modeled). \S9's new "key custody at scale" bullet is the implicit retraction; a hostile reviewer will quote both sentences side by side.

The Omega/EIP-7702/IBC/ZKsync citations (\S8 line 7, line 11, line 21) read as compositional namedrops rather than positioning deltas. The IBC citation in particular ("the Cosmos IBC protocol carries cross-consensus messages as its primary contribution") states what IBC does, not how Chio's treaty differs; a PC member working in distributed systems will write "the IBC comparison is one sentence and does not address relay-and-trust-assumption inheritance, light-client requirements, or replay-protection windows."

The single objection a co-reviewer is most likely to write: "Two of four headline theorems are `rfl`; the authors disclose this in \S4 but build the central rhetorical move (Hart's rule of recognition, conclusion) on those two. The paper sells a constructive jurisprudence result that the formal artifact does not bear."

Highest-priority follow-up: rewrite the \S7 Hart paragraph and the conclusion's "constructive instance" sentence so the Hartian framing rests on the two load-bearing theorems (`treaty_admission_iff_predicate_intersection` and `treaty_admission_stable_under_ladder_floor`) -- i.e., rule of recognition as the *intersection* predicate, not the *amendment* predicate -- and explicitly name the amendment side as type-discipline rather than recognition theory.

## Persona 2: PL/formal-methods skeptic

The most damaging new observation is that `ConstitutionalDelta` is essentially unconstructable for any non-trivial polity, which makes §4's "type-level invariant" rhetorically strong but operationally vacuous -- downstream of the decidability category error the polish-diff already logged, but distinct and not yet recorded.

`BackwardRefines new old` is a `Prop` of shape `∀ r : String, constitutionAllows new r = true → constitutionAllows old r = true` (Intersection.lean:80-82). Constructing a closed term requires reasoning about arbitrary `ReceiptId → Bool` closures. Because predicates are opaque functions rather than syntactic terms with a denotation, real callers can inhabit `BackwardRefines` only via (a) trivially-empty constitutions, (b) identity `K' = K`, or (c) a proof manually written in Lean by a human with source access to both predicate sets. The paper sells "votes on theorems, not bytes" as a runtime discipline; the artifact provides a discipline exercisable only inside the Lean source tree. Until a syntactic predicate ADT with a denotation exists, §4's "cannot be constructed by the well-typed runtime path" is *uninformative* -- nothing else can be either.

Three mathlib-reviewer flags not yet in the polish-diff:

1. `enactAmendment (_delta : ConstitutionalDelta) := .enacted` (line 103) underscores its argument. The function is a constant. The pass-4 narrative -- "the type system enforces that `.enacted` cannot be produced without the refinement witness" -- is defensible only because `Prop` is proof-irrelevant and erased; a reviewer not steeped in DTT reads three lines and writes "this function ignores its proof obligation."

2. `BackwardRefines` mixes `Prop`-level `∀` with `Bool`-level equality (`= true`) rather than going through `Decidable` or `decide`. For a mathlib reviewer this telegraphs that the `Decidable`/`Prop` boundary has not been thought through.

3. `simp [Bool.and_assoc]` (line 123) closes the headline theorem by reassociation. The polish-diff records "all four are unfoldings"; not recorded is that `treatyPredicateIntersection` and `treatyAdmits` are written as manually-distinct conjunctions of the same six Booleans (lines 60-72). The theorem literally states that the author wrote `A ∧ B ∧ C ∧ D ∧ E ∧ F` two different ways.

On §4 after pass 5: the three-obligations paragraph reads as agenda, but sits in tension with §1 bullet 2 -- if trajectory invariants for amendment are open, the amendment-side type-level invariant should not be called load-bearing two pages earlier. On Hart: the construction does not instantiate Hart's rule of recognition. Hart requires recognition by *officials as social practice*; the Lean model has no analog of officialdom -- only `scope.contains r && constitutionAllows ...`. The framing is decorative until the paper either supplies an explicit recognition predicate or softens §7's "constructive instance" to "machine-evaluable analog."

Highest-priority follow-up: build a syntactic `Predicate` ADT with a `denote : Predicate → ReceiptId → Bool` interpreter, so `BackwardRefines` becomes `∀ p₁ ∈ new.predicates, ∃ p₂ ∈ old.predicates, p₁ ⊑ p₂` over a decidable subsumption relation. This converts the type-level invariant from "vacuously enforced because unconstructable" to "enforced because checkable on real inputs," and is the same move Cedar made.

## Persona 6: Naive smart reader (role: technical aide to a US senator briefing on whether this paper changes any regulatory question)

I picked persona (b): Senate staffer. My boss sits on a committee that cares about AI accountability, sanctions enforcement against AI services, and whether agent systems can be made auditable enough to assign liability. I have a CS undergrad but I am not a Lean person.

1. Elevator pitch test. After the abstract my one-sentence read is: "These people built a way to make AI agents emit cryptographic receipts whose admissibility is checked against machine-readable rules, and they proved two small lemmas about how those rules compose." That is plausibly useful. But "polity," "constitution," and "citizenship roster" set off alarm bells. The vocabulary reads like the authors want to claim agents form their own jurisdictions, which is exactly the rhetoric we would push back on in a hearing.

2. Buried lede. If I were writing a memo, I would NOT quote the polity-triple language. I would quote two things: (i) "the EU AI Act General-Purpose AI Code of Practice came into force in August 2025 with transparency and safety chapters that name the logging and incident-record obligations Chio's receipts are shaped to discharge" (S7) and (ii) "no current attestation framework (SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCAOB, sectoral) cites Lean-attested receipt evidence; until a recognized framework maps receipts to existing control language, Chio is an evidence producer with no accredited consumer" (S9). Quote (ii) IS the headline for my boss: technology exists, regulators have not yet specified how to consume it. That is a clean ask -- have NIST or a sectoral regulator write the mapping. The paper's actual abstract buries this completely.

3. Trust test. Feels honest in S9 (the limitations table and bullet list are unusually candid -- "no end-user surface," "regulator accreditation gap," "override authority for false negatives") and in the benchmark "withheld" rows. Feels evasive in S1/S4/S10 where the polity framing dominates. The conclusion's "Hartian rule of recognition" sentence is the part that would make a regulator suspicious: the authors keep saying "we have no authority over physical persons or external courts" while simultaneously deploying the vocabulary of states. Pick one. Also: the paper never tells me what happens when the kernel operator IS the bad actor (sanctioned vendor, foreign-state-controlled vendor, compromised host). The threat model in S4 assumes the agent is hostile but the kernel is trusted; that is exactly inverted from the regulator's worry.

4. So-what for my role. Modest. This does NOT settle whether agent receipts are sufficient for liability assignment, does NOT propose statutory language, and does NOT identify a regulator that has agreed to accept this evidence. It IS useful as one input to a NIST workstream and as a reference point if Treasury/OFAC ever asks "could AI service providers be required to emit machine-checkable compliance receipts?" -- the post-paper answer is "yes, here is an existence proof." That is real but small. It does not change any pending bill.

5. One thing I wish it said. A concrete one-page worked example of a regulator-facing use case: a hypothetical sanctions-screening or HIPAA-export predicate, a receipt that would be admitted, a receipt that would be denied, and what an OFAC or HHS examiner would actually look at. Without that I cannot brief anyone on what "machine-checkable governance" feels like in a recognizable enforcement context.

Highest-priority follow-up: add a regulator-facing worked example (one page, one named predicate, one allow receipt, one deny receipt, one regulator-side replay command) so that a non-Lean reader can see what programmable sovereignty actually does in a recognizable enforcement scenario -- and reframe the abstract around "evidence producer for existing regulators" rather than around polities, citizens, and constitutions.

## Iteration summary

Convergent across hostile + senator's aide: the abstract's polity rhetoric is in direct tension with \S9's portability and regulator-accreditation bullets, and the buried lede is "evidence producer for existing regulators" not "constructive jurisprudence." Divergent: hostile reviewer wants the Hart claim narrowed to the load-bearing theorems (intersection + ladder stability); PL skeptic wants a syntactic `Predicate` ADT with `denote` (the Cedar move) to make `BackwardRefines` decidable; senator's aide wants a regulator-facing worked example. The three follow-ups stack rather than conflict: narrow the Hart claim, build the Predicate ADT, add the worked example.
