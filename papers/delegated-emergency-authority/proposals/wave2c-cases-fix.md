# Wave 2C: §3 case-study fixes

This log records targeted rewrites applied to `sections/03-cases.tex` to address case-specific substantive errors identified by Wave 1A (constitutional history), Wave 1B (national security), and Wave 1C (intermediary liability and EU data protection). Scope was limited to `sections/03-cases.tex`; no other files were touched.

## §3.1 Weimar Article 48: the canonical case

(i) Changes. Three "non-historian gloss" problems flagged by Wave 1A were addressed. First, the path from Article 48 to the Enabling Act was rewritten to run through the Reichstag Fire Decree's *Schutzhaft* arrests of approximately eighty-one KPD deputies (and harassment of SPD deputies) that made the two-thirds threshold reachable, rather than through a generalized "normalization of governance by emergency decree." Second, the Reichstag's Article 48(3) suspension power is now framed as politically conditional on SPD *Tolerierung* throughout 1930-33, with Hindenburg's July 1930 and 1932 dissolutions removing the occasion for suspension votes. Third, Brüning, Papen, and Schleicher are now disaggregated: Brüning's legalistic deflationary emergency-budget program, Papen's anti-parliamentary turn including the July 1932 *Preußenschlag*, and Schleicher's *Querfront* attempt. A new paragraph engages *Preußen contra Reich* (Staatsgerichtshof, 25 October 1932), the case in which Article 48's limits were litigated and partially struck down; the paragraph narrows the structural claim to "the structural absence made the ratchet structurally available," not "textually entailed." The "case study against which the Article's structural claim is most defensible" was changed to "the case study where the structural absence is most legible," preserving the weak reading. Wave 1A finding 4's softening recommendation ("decrees of that form unconstructible, although the political pressure ... would have remained") was applied.

(ii) Findings addressed. Wave 1A findings 2 (historiography), 3 (primary-source discipline), 4 (strong-vs-weak ratchet claim), and 5 (non-historian gloss).

## §3.2 Section 230's incentive shadow and the DMCA 512 takedown regime

(i) Changes. Implemented Wave 1C's Option B (preserve case but reframe). Subsection heading was changed from "Section 230 and the takedown ecosystem" to "Section 230's incentive shadow and the DMCA 512 takedown regime." Opening paragraph now explicitly concedes the framing is one the Article advances against the consensus of the Section 230 academy. Section 230(c)(1) (liability shield, ex post) and Section 230(c)(2) (good-Samaritan carve-out) are separated from DMCA 512 explicitly; DMCA §512(g) counter-notice is now characterized as a partial typed rollback witness already present in the adjacent copyright regime. Section 230(c)(2) is acknowledged as "already a hedged takedown regime of a particular form." The structural claim is reframed: Section 230's incentive shadow produces self-regulatory takedowns that lack a structural rollback witness, even though §512(g) supplies a partial counter-example in the copyright-takedown adjacent regime. Goldman, Citron & Wittes, and Keller are now cited as the named scholars whose positions the Article engages.

(ii) Findings addressed. Wave 1C findings 1 (category error), 2 (academy engagement), and 6 (tells: no (c)(2) treatment, DMCA conflation).

## §3.3 The 2001 AUMF: a quarter-century of unbounded authorization

(i) Changes. The "structural rather than political" framing was hedged for internal consistency with the Wave 2B §1 fix. The trajectory now cites the 2018 Section 1264 Report (naming Afghanistan, Iraq, Syria, Yemen, Somalia, Libya, the Philippines, with Niger and Kenya in earlier transmittals), CRS R43983, and the 50 U.S.C. §1543 War Powers Resolution transmittals; Jaffer is retained but repositioned for the associated-forces legal-theory critique rather than the 2024 head count. The bipartisan repeal-and-replace record was added: Kaine-Young (S.1228), Lee-Murphy, the Corker-Kaine 2018 framework, and the 2023 repeal of the 1991 and 2002 Iraq AUMFs (signed by Biden, leaving 2001 intact). The structural reading is now explicitly framed as supplementary to, not displacing, the political account. The closing paragraph acknowledges that the AUMF case is the one where the political account is strongest and the structural reading must ride alongside the political one.

(ii) Findings addressed. Wave 1B findings 1 (eight-theater citation swap) and 2 (structural-vs-political inconsistency).

## §3.5 FISA Section 702: programmatic acquisition and its emergency sub-provision

(i) Changes. Subsection heading was renamed from "FISA Section 702: emergency surveillance authorizations" to "FISA Section 702: programmatic acquisition and its emergency sub-provision." Opening paragraph disambiguates Section 702 (programmatic certification regime, reviewed annually under §1881a(j)) from §1881a(c)(2) (the seventy-two-hour sub-provision). The case study is now explicitly about the sub-provision's interaction with the broader §702 retention and querying framework. Specific FISC opinions added: Bates 2011 upstream (MCT), Collyer 2017 about-collection, Boasberg 2018 querying procedures, Contreras 2022 FBI query compliance. PCLOB reports added: July 2014, January 2017 Recommendations Assessment, September 2023. Reauthorization legislation added: FAA Reauth 2017 (Pub. L. 115-118) and RISAA 2024 (Pub. L. 118-49). A new "existing rollback-adjacent mechanisms" paragraph names §1881a(i)(3) FISC destruction orders, §1809(a) criminal bar, five-year retention defaults, and post-2018 querying procedures, and explains why these are partial. Case-law engagement added: *Clapper v. Amnesty Int'l USA* (568 U.S. 398) for standing, *United States v. Hasbajrami* (2d Cir. 2019) for §702 querying of U.S.-person information.

(ii) Findings addressed. Wave 1B findings 3 (FISC and PCLOB specificity), 4 (Section 702 / sub-provision conflation), 5 (existing rollback-adjacent mechanisms), and 6 (missing public-record items: reauthorizations, Clapper, Hasbajrami).
