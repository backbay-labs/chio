# Wave 1C: Intermediary liability + EU data protection audit

## 1. The Section 230 / DMCA 512 conflation

The introduction (lines 71-77 of `01-introduction.tex`) states: "Section~230 of the Communications Decency Act, although not in its primary form a takedown regime, operates in conjunction with the Digital Millennium Copyright Act's notice-and-takedown procedure ... to produce content removals that share the structural shape of a time-bounded executive act." Section 3.2 doubles down at lines 92-94: "The combined effect is a takedown regime in which removal is structurally a time-bounded executive act of a private intermediary, with no statutory mechanism that requires the intermediary to construct a path back to the prior state."

This conflates two regimes with fundamentally different statutory architecture. Section 230(c)(1) is a liability shield: it operates ex post, when a plaintiff sues, and immunizes a publisher from being treated as the speaker. It does not authorize, require, or even mention takedown. DMCA 512 is the actual notice-and-takedown regime, and unlike Section 230 it does contain a structurally relevant rollback path: the 17 U.S.C. § 512(g) counter-notice procedure obligates the service provider to restore content within 10-14 business days of a counter-notice unless the complainant files suit. The paper's "time-bounded executive act" framing therefore fits DMCA 512 more naturally than Section 230, and DMCA 512 already has the rollback witness the paper claims is structurally absent.

Proposed rewrite: separate the two paragraphs. Drop Section 230 from the introduction's enumerated cases, or recast it explicitly as the immunity that frames the platform's incentive structure. The case study should be reframed as "DMCA 512 and the platform-incentives shadow of Section 230," with the structural claim located in (a) the asymmetric incentive Section 230(c)(2) creates and (b) the friction of the § 512(g) counter-notice as an underutilized but real rollback path. The current framing reads as a category error a Goldman or a Keller reviewer would dispatch in a sentence.

## 2. Citron / Goldman / Keller engagement

The §3.2 footnote at line 94-98 invokes Keller, and lines 121-125 gesture toward Goldman and Citron, but each is cited for the wrong proposition. The paper should acknowledge that the three named scholars converge against treating Section 230 as a takedown regime, though from different directions. Goldman, "Why Section 230 Is Better Than the First Amendment," 95 Notre Dame L. Rev. Reflection 33 (2019), expressly defends 230 as a non-takedown immunity. Keller, "Empirical Evidence of Over-Removal by Internet Companies Under Intermediary Liability Laws," Stanford CIS (2015), uses the over-removal data to argue against treating immunity withdrawal as a takedown lever. Citron & Wittes, "The Internet Will Not Break: Denying Bad Samaritans § 230 Immunity," 86 Fordham L. Rev. 401 (2017), argue for narrowing the shield but on bad-Samaritan grounds, not on the irreversibility framing the paper deploys.

The paper currently invokes Citron and Goldman as objections to its grammar, but the more honest framing is that all three would dispute the structural premise. The §3.2 case-study introduction should add a sentence: "The framing of Section 230 as bearing structural similarity to a delegated emergency authority is one this Article advances against the consensus of the Section 230 academy, which has, in differing directions, resisted the characterization of 230 as a takedown regime [cite Goldman 2019, Citron & Wittes 2017, Keller 2015]."

## 3. The GDPR Article 17 / CJEU case law gap

§3.4 at lines 134-141 cites *Google Spain* once and otherwise treats Article 17 in the abstract. The case law that actually operationalizes the right to erasure is missing. The §3.4 paragraph at line 137 cites only the foundational decision; it omits *GC and Others v. CNIL* (Case C-136/17, ECLI:EU:C:2019:773, 24 September 2019), which clarified the scope of de-referencing for special-category data and explicitly held that the balance is re-evaluable; *Google LLC v. CNIL* (Case C-507/17, 24 September 2019), which held the right to de-referencing does not extend globally; and *Lindqvist* (Case C-101/01, 6 November 2003), the prior-regime case on processing personal data that established the threshold definition the GDPR inherited. The paper also misses *TU and RE v. Google LLC* (Case C-460/20, 8 December 2022), which addresses re-listing when allegedly inaccurate content is shown to be accurate.

Without these, §3.4 reads as if the author has read Article 17's text but not the case law that operationalizes it. The paragraph at lines 142-151 should be rewritten to acknowledge that the CJEU has, since *Google Spain*, developed a substantial body of jurisprudence on the balance the controller and the data protection authority must strike. A single citation to *Google Spain* in 2026 is insufficient.

## 4. Required additions: Lynskey, Bygrave, Article 29 WP / EDPB

The bibliography lists Mantelero and Kuner. The paper needs at minimum: Orla Lynskey, *The Foundations of EU Data Protection Law* (Oxford 2015), particularly Chapters 6-7 on the right to be forgotten as a structural element rather than a remedy; Lee A. Bygrave, *Data Privacy Law: An International Perspective* (Oxford, 2d ed. 2014) on the architecture; and the Article 29 Working Party Guidelines on the Implementation of *Google Spain* (WP 225, 26 November 2014), now succeeded by EDPB Guidelines 5/2019 on the criteria of the Right to be Forgotten in the search engines cases. The EDPB guidance is essential because it codifies the re-evaluability and the controller's ongoing obligation: the paper's claim that the order is "structurally irreversible at the moment of its admission" is directly in tension with the WP/EDPB framework that contemplates revisiting. Lynskey is the most essential single addition because she frames the right structurally rather than transactionally.

## 5. The "irreversible by construction" overclaim

The introduction at lines 66-67 states: "A search-engine index from which a result has been removed is not, in any practical sense, restored to its prior shape by the lapse of the order." §3.4 at lines 147-150 repeats: "The deletion is, as a practical matter, irreversible: even if the order is later set aside by judicial review, the data is not, in general, restored." This is stronger than CJEU practice supports. The "manifestly inadequate, irrelevant, no longer relevant, or excessive" standard from *Google Spain* paragraph 93 is re-evaluable: a data subject who becomes a public figure (e.g., stands for election) can lose the protection prospectively; the controller can re-index. *GC and Others* makes the time-sensitivity explicit. The Article 19(3) GDPR obligation on the controller to inform recipients of erasure, and Article 17(3)(a)'s public-interest exception, both contemplate ongoing reassessment.

Proposed hedge: replace "is not, in any practical sense, restored" with "is not, on the regulation's default operation, restored, although the underlying balance is re-evaluable and the controller may re-index where the supporting facts change." The point the paper actually wants -- that the moment-of-admission grammar is missing -- survives the hedge.

## 6. Tells the paper has not engaged the internet-law academy

Three tells will read as non-engagement. First, the §3.2 framing at lines 92-94 (quoted above) treats DMCA 512 and Section 230 as a single regime; an internet-law reader will object that 512(g) counter-notice is precisely the typed rollback witness the paper claims is absent. Second, §3.2 nowhere mentions Section 230(c)(2) -- the "good Samaritan" carve-out that explicitly authorizes good-faith restriction of access to objectionable material. (c)(2) is structurally already a hedged takedown regime; the paper's "no rollback path" claim is in tension with the carve-out's existence and with the body of case law construing it (e.g., *Zango v. Kaspersky*, *e-ventures Worldwide v. Google*). Third, §3.4 treats Article 17 as if it were the only EU erasure mechanism, ignoring the e-Privacy Directive 2002/58/EC (cookie/tracking erasure), the Law Enforcement Directive 2016/680 Article 16 (which differs from GDPR 17 in important respects), and the DSA Article 16 notice-and-action regime now operative as of February 2024. The DSA in particular supplies a partial counterexample: it contains structured trusted-flagger and counter-notice mechanisms that look more like the typed rollback the paper proposes than the paper acknowledges.

## Bottom line

In current form neither section survives a hostile internet-law reviewer. §3.2 has a category error (Section 230 conflated with DMCA 512) that any specialist will catch in the first paragraph; §3.4 has a case-law gap (single citation to *Google Spain*, nothing on the subsequent CJEU line, no EDPB guidance) that signals the author has read the regulation without reading the jurisprudence. Of the two, §3.4 is more salvageable: the structural claim about irreversibility-at-admission is defensible once hedged for re-evaluability, and the fix is additive (cite *GC and Others*, *Google v. CNIL*, EDPB 5/2019, add Lynskey and Bygrave). §3.2 needs structural surgery: either drop Section 230 as a case and reframe around DMCA 512 with the (c)(2) good-Samaritan carve-out treated honestly, or rewrite the framing as "Section 230's incentive shadow produces takedown-like behavior in DMCA 512 and platform self-regulation," and concede up front that the Section 230 academy disputes the characterization.
